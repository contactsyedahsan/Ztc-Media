The Goat Life — A Real Story of Survival in the Saudi Arabian Desert

How one man's desperate search for work led him into modern-day slavery in the Gulf — and the systemic failures that allowed it to happen.

Z
ZTC Investigates
May 2025 Long-form Investigation
14 min read
Key Facts
  • The kafala (sponsorship) system ties millions of migrant workers to a single employer, stripping them of freedom of movement.
  • Saudi Arabia hosts approximately 13.4 million migrant workers — nearly 40% of the total population.
  • Pakistan is one of the largest sources of Gulf migrant labour, with an estimated 3.2 million workers in the region.
  • Aadu Jeevitham (The Goat Life) is based on the true story of Najeeb Muhammad, a Malayali migrant worker who was enslaved by a Saudi farmer for years.

In the late 1990s, a man boarded a flight from Kerala, India, with a single bag, a work visa, and the hope of sending money home to his family. What followed was years of captivity in the Saudi desert — tending livestock, with no documents, no wages, and no way out. His name was Najeeb Muhammad. His story is not an anomaly.

ZTC Media's investigation into the conditions of South Asian migrant labour in the Gulf reveals a pattern that has persisted for decades — one enabled by the kafala system, inadequate oversight from home governments, and the economic desperation that drives millions of people to take risks they barely understand.

The Promise That Lures Millions

For families living in rural Pakistan, Bangladesh, India, and Nepal, a Gulf work visa represents something rare: a path out of poverty that doesn't require a university degree. Recruitment agencies, often working in informal networks, promise skilled and semi-skilled positions — as drivers, construction workers, domestic helpers, or farm labourers. The promised salaries can be transformative for families who survive on less than $5 a day.

But the contracts workers sign before departure are frequently not the contracts that await them. Many workers arrive to find their role, salary, or employer has changed entirely. Their passports are confiscated — a practice that remains widespread despite being technically illegal in most Gulf states — and they are left with no way to leave.

"He had come to the desert to build a better life. Instead, the desert swallowed him whole."

— Benyamin, Author of Aadujeevitham

The Kafala System: Architecture of Control

The kafala (sponsorship) system is the legal framework that governs migrant labour across most of the Gulf Cooperation Council states, including Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Oman. Under this system, a migrant worker's legal right to reside and work in a Gulf country is tied entirely to a single private sponsor — their employer.

What this means in practice is stark: the worker cannot change jobs, cannot leave the country, and cannot seek alternative housing without explicit permission from the sponsor. Abuse, wage theft, or dangerous working conditions become impossible to escape without risking deportation or imprisonment — which the employer can initiate at any time.

Why Reform Has Been Slow

Gulf governments have faced sustained international pressure — from the International Labour Organization, from human rights groups, and from the media — to reform or abolish kafala. Qatar's labour reforms ahead of the 2022 World Cup attracted significant attention, with officials announcing an end to the exit permit requirement and a new minimum wage. In practice, implementation has been uneven, and rights groups continue to document widespread violations.

Saudi Arabia announced its own Vision 2030 reforms, which include pledges to modernize the labour market. Yet workers' advocacy organizations report that the structural power imbalance remains — and that workers who attempt to use the new complaint channels often face retaliation.

Najeeb's Story — and the Thousands Like Him

The Goat Life is based on the account of Najeeb Muhammad, but it is far from isolated. ZTC Media spoke to community advocates working with returned migrants across Pakistan's Punjab province who described receiving similar accounts regularly — workers who spent years in conditions of forced labour with no ability to contact family or seek help.

The common thread across nearly every account was the moment of passport confiscation. Once the document was taken, the worker's vulnerability was complete. Without identification, they could not travel, could not report to police, and could not be formally employed elsewhere. Many described being afraid that if they contacted authorities, they would be deported as illegal workers — even though they had arrived legally.

"They were afraid of the very system that should have protected them. That fear is not accidental — it is by design."

— Human Rights Researcher, source protected

What Meaningful Change Requires

Researchers and advocates interviewed by ZTC Media consistently identified several key interventions: the complete abolition of passport confiscation backed by criminal penalties; a shift from employer-tied visas to sector-tied permits that allow workers to change jobs freely; and robust bilateral agreements between sending and receiving countries that establish enforceable minimum standards.

Pakistan's Overseas Employment Corporation and its equivalents in other sending countries also bear responsibility. Workers are frequently inadequately briefed on their rights, the contracts they sign are not verified against the actual conditions of the role, and there are minimal support mechanisms when things go wrong abroad.

The story of Najeeb Muhammad is now a film, a book, and a moment of cultural reckoning. But for millions of workers currently in the Gulf under similar conditions, the reckoning is still to come.
